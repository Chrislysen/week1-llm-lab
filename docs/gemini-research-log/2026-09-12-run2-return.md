Analysis of Memory Supersession and Retraction in Multi-Agent Dialogue Systems

Focus Block: Corpora for Executable Multi-Agent Retraction

The evaluation of supersession, retraction, and revocation within multi-agent memory architectures necessitates dialogue corpora in which proposals are explicitly tabled, debated, rejected, and resolved into concrete, executable outcomes. While standard benchmarks often evaluate passive information tracking, multi-agent strategic alignment requires environments characterized by transactional conflict and convergence. A systematic review of available datasets reveals several foundational corpora capable of supporting this paradigm.

The CaSiNo (Campsite Negotiation Dialogues) dataset provides 1,030 multi-turn negotiation dialogues in English [VERIFIED; cite: 86; researchgate 350512648, Abstract]. Participants assume the roles of campsite neighbors negotiating the allocation of food, water, and firewood, yielding highly structured, executable outcomes bounded by quantifiable resource constraints [VERIFIED; cite: 86; Section 2]. The dataset is released under the CC BY 4.0 license [VERIFIED; cite: 92; Public Full-text 1]. Its strict proposal-and-rejection sequences make it an optimal testbed for evaluating whether an agent mistakenly resurrects a rejected allocation constraint due to memory retrieval failures. Furthermore, extensions such as NegotiationToM leverage the CaSiNo corpus to evaluate theory-of-mind by tracking evolving mental states—specifically desires, beliefs, and intentions—across sequential dialogue turns, mapping directly to the tracking of superseded transactional facts [VERIFIED; cite: 220; arXiv:2404.13627v1, Section 3.3].

Similarly, the Deal or No Deal dataset captures outcome-level bargaining wherein agents divide randomized sets of items to maximize utility [VERIFIED; cite: 103; mdpi 2079-9292/15/10/2039, Abstract]. The corpus comprises 5,808 dialogues and is frequently released under non-commercial academic licenses (e.g., CC BY-NC 4.0) [INFERRED; cite: 95; ICLR 2025 proceedings, References]. Because the dialogue inherently concludes with an executable allocation and features rigid proposal-then-rejection dynamics, it provides a high-fidelity environment for testing whether a memory layer correctly isolates valid commitments from revoked offers [VERIFIED; cite: 104; arXiv:2603.14066v1, Text].

Beyond pure negotiation, multi-party meeting transcripts feature extensive proposal-then-rejection exchanges annotated with granular dialogue acts, making them highly adaptable for evaluating memory retraction algorithms. The AMI Meeting Corpus records simulated multi-party project meetings (specifically, the design of a remote control) comprising 100 hours of synchronized multi-modal data [VERIFIED; cite: 73; researchgate 220147804, Abstract]. Released under CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 [VERIFIED; cite: 67; researchgate 395526221, Metadata], the dataset includes explicit dialogue act annotations representing the dynamics of group decision-making, utilizing tags such as "Action-directive," "Concord," "Discord," and "Negative" [VERIFIED; cite: 240; scispace 1z87zcma3q, Section IV]. These annotations effectively isolate temporal markers where a proposal is tabled and subsequently rejected by the group. This enables researchers to evaluate whether a planning agent later hallucinates the rejected concept as an accepted group consensus due to memory compaction errors. Additionally, the ICSI Meeting Corpus captures naturalistic, multi-party academic and engineering meetings across 75 sessions [VERIFIED; cite: 71; cs.cmu.edu laskowskiTHESIS, Text]. Utilizing the MRDA (Meeting Recorder Dialog Act) taxonomy, it tracks acceptances, rejections, and disagreements regarding proposed technical decisions [VERIFIED; cite: 234; researchgate 329628095, Text], though adapting it to yield cleanly executable deterministic outcomes may require secondary annotation pipelines [INFERRED; cite: 243; researchgate 228910338, Section 3.5].

Task 3: Adversarial Review of Memory-Design Evaluation

Objection 1 — Estimand Ambiguity (Recognition vs. Application). The evaluation may measure State Resolution (stating that the item was rejected) rather than Implicit Policy Adaptation (organically omitting it from downstream planning). The experimental memory design might act as a negative constraint in the context window.
Required Control: a two-phase framework isolating implicit probing, with naturalistic queries mentioning neither the original proposal nor the rejection.

Objection 2 — Confounding Retrieval with Post-Retrieval Assembly. The bottleneck in conflict resolution is frequently the post-retrieval assembly phase rather than storage or retrieval. The gain may be a whole-pipeline effect from prompt formats or temperature.
Required Control: an ablated baseline with deterministic assembly operating evenly over control and experimental stores.

Objection 3 — Ceiling/Floor Effects from Fixed-Length Plans. A dense memory might crowd out rejected items by token-limit truncation or attention dilution; the improvement could be context-window saturation.
Required Control: matched-budget retrieval, identical token volume in both arms, plus multiple plan-length requirements (3-step vs 10-step).

Objection 4 — Corpus Artefacts and Lexical Templating. With synthesized or heavily templated dialogue (e.g. incrementing serial numbers as in FactConsolidation), the model may regex-filter templated phrasing; this evaluates index sorting rather than semantic comprehension of revocation.
Required Control: a naturally occurring, unstructured multi-party corpus with high linguistic variance (e.g. CaSiNo), plus an adversarial Premise Resistance arm using misleading queries that presuppose validity.

Objection 5 — Decider-Size Effects. Complex designs rely on the orchestrating LLM's reasoning; the improvement might only manifest on a frontier model parsing temporal metadata.
Required Control: identical protocol across 7B local, mid-tier open weights and frontier proprietary models; a consistent delta at all tiers.

Objection 6 — True by Construction (The Hard-Deletion Tautology). If the design physically deletes rejected facts, the agent cannot include them; that is an algorithmic tautology.
Required Control: a "Soft Revocation" arm where the rejected item is retained but tagged with invalidation metadata, proving the agent parses supersession metadata to exclude the proposal.

Task 5: Frontier Experiments

Experiment 1: The Negotiation Re-injection Trap. CaSiNo; two agents through a shared memory layer; Agent A proposes 3 water for 2 firewood at Turn 2, Agent B rejects; at Turn 8 Agent A presents a final agreement draft subtly re-injecting the clause. Compare flat vector RAG against temporal graph memory for Agent B. Outcome: Premise Resistance, the rate at which Agent B flags the re-injected clause. Requirements: CaSiNo; open-weights mid-tier models (e.g. Llama-3-8B). Breakthrough: bitemporal architectures reaching near-100% rejection-enforcement against adversarial re-injection.

Experiment 2: Soft-Revocation Distraction Penalty Across Model Scales. Hard-delete pipeline against a soft-revocation pipeline with invalid_at tags; contexts fed to 7B–100B+ models generating an action plan from an AMI transcript where a technical proposal was rejected. Outcome: Unsafe Execution Rate. Breakthrough: a scaling law identifying the parameter threshold at which models stop being distracted by soft-revoked text.

Experiment 3: Latency-Induced Stale Propagation in Shared Graphs. Throttle the sync layer in a StateFuse-style shared memory; Agent 1 revokes a constraint while Agent 2 is queried to execute a dependent plan; vary the gap from 10ms to 500ms. Outcome: Stale Propagation Rate. Breakthrough: a pre-computation dependency lock reducing it to zero without crippling throughput.

Experiment 4: Implicit Consensus Invalidation. Adapt STALE's IPA mechanism to multi-party dynamics on Deal or No Deal; update item market values via system prompts so the agreed distribution becomes inequitable with no explicit rejection. Outcome: Group State Resolution Accuracy. Breakthrough: a post-generation audit pipeline raising adaptation success above 90%.

Experiment 5: Decoupling Semantic Compaction from Temporal Revocation. Test whether pipelines such as Mem0 v3 ADD-only distinguish conversational repetition from conversational supersession. Outcome: internal graph size and state accuracy. Breakthrough: an extraction schema categorising 99% of updates into correct AUDN bins from pragmatic dialogue acts rather than lexical similarity.
